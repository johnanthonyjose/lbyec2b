IWhy this exists

Your program can be produced by someone or something other than you. Your account of why it is built that way, written in a room, about a change you were not warned of, cannot.

So the exercise is assessed as an audit. You implement it; then you explain and defend it under supervision. The faculty-in-charge forms a judgment from the whole set of evidence, not from the program alone.

The principle

Everything you claim about what you built, you write in the room.

Not a detector. This is not designed to catch AI use. It is designed so the question seldom needs asking — where the work is not yours, there is very little to say, and no allegation is needed for that to show.

IIThe shape of the activity

The audit runs in one of two modes. Which one applies is in the panel above. Switch between them in the figure to see what actually changes.

Mode A is the default. Mode B exists only for exercises too large to finish in one sitting.
The rule
  1. A dry run of the whole format is held once per term, before the first audited exercise. It is not graded; attendance is required.
  2. In Mode A, an incomplete program does not forfeit the Responses. You answer against the design you attempted, working or not.
  3. In Mode B, commit progressively as you work, not in a single commit at the end.
  4. Evaluations are accomplished at home, within two (2) days, through Canvas.
  5. One re-sit is available on request, no reason required.
Absence. You re-sit. Absence does not by itself result in a grade of zero (0.0).

IIIYour team, and who you work with

You belong to an Audit Team of six, fixed for the term and deliberately not the same people as your Project Team. Within it, everyone probes one classmate and is probed by a different one, so nobody can trade favours.

Select yourself in the figure to see your four relationships.

Rotated each time. The assignments turn by one position for every audited exercise, so no pairing repeats.

IVWriting a Modification

This is the part most often done badly, and it carries marks of its own. A Modification must change a constraint of the problem — something the original solution relied on — and not merely a parameter within it.

A parameter change asks your classmate to edit a line. A constraint change asks them to rethink a decision. Only the second tells anyone whether they understood their own design.

What is graded is the single sentence naming which part of their design you expect to break — not the length of the modification.
The rule

A valid Modification alters an assumption about input validity, a limit on scale or memory, the shape or arrival order of the data, a new failure condition, or the removal of an assumption the original relied upon. Changing a literal, renaming an identifier, or requiring extra output shall not be credited.

Twelve percent of the activity is the Modification you write for someone else.

VWriting a Response

You answer two Modifications, fifteen minutes each, offline, about your own program. Each answer takes the same four steps — the repetition is deliberate, since the second answer is what shows the first was not a lucky guess.

Each Response, in order
  1. What breaks in your program under this Modification, and why.
  2. The minimum change that would address it.
  3. The revised algorithm, as pseudo-code.
  4. One thing that could still go wrong afterwards.

Pseudo-code, flowcharts and diagrams are all accepted. Handwriting, spelling and command of English are not graded — only the reasoning. You may record your Response aloud instead, within the same time limit, without giving a reason.

On the Plan. A prediction of difficulty that proves wrong is not penalised. A wrong prediction you afterwards account for is worth more than a cautious one that says nothing. Write what you honestly expect.

VIWhere the marks come from

The distribution is fixed for every audited exercise of the same mode. Only the weight of the activity as a whole varies, and it is in the panel on the first page.

Notice what switching to Mode A does: it moves the program itself from unobserved to observed work.

What gets read

Published in advance, so nothing is assessed that you were not told about: your Plan and the account of what changed; your program; the Modification you formulated; your two Responses; the observations of the two members who evaluated you; and, in Mode B only, your commit history.

Where the commit history is read, it is used only to raise a question for clarification, never to set a mark directly. There is nothing to gain by inflating commits.

No student grades another student. Peer evaluations are evidence for the faculty-in-charge, and are subject to review.

VIIIn the room

Conduct of the session
  • Permitted: your own repository, read-only; the exercise; writing materials. Answers may be written on paper.
  • Not permitted: internet access, any AI assistant, mobile telephones, or any file other than your own repository.
Phones are kept away for the whole session, in both modes.

VIIIArtificial intelligence, and concerns

The course policy and the Student Handbook provisions on academic honesty apply in full. Beyond that, the mechanics do the work rather than surveillance.

Raising a concern
  1. Any member may raise a concern, which must cite something specific and verifiable. A general impression shall not be entertained.
  2. No incentive or credit of any kind attaches to raising a concern.
  3. A concern is resolved by a conversation of about five (5) minutes with the faculty-in-charge about your own code. Ordinarily the matter ends there.
Why no reward? Paying someone to find a problem destroys their judgment about whether the problem is there. Concerns are unpaid for the same reason auditors are not paid by result.

IXOther provisions

  1. Default of a classmate. Where no Modification is formulated for you, the faculty-in-charge issues one. No student is prejudiced by another's default.
  2. Failure to perform a role. Failure to formulate an assigned Modification or accomplish an assigned evaluation earns no credit for that component, and does not affect the member who was to receive it.
  3. Reconsideration. May be sought within one (1) week of release, during consultation hours. The evidence relied upon shall be shown to you.
Queries may be raised during consultation hours or through Canvas — kindly before the dry run, so the mechanics are settled in advance.

XGuide for Team Leaders

Published to the whole class, so every member knows what the Team Leader may and may not do.

You are not a grader. You make sure the evidence your team produces is complete and properly supported before it reaches the faculty-in-charge, who evaluates you on exactly that. You are audited on your own exercise like everyone else; your Deputy reviews your submissions.

Your week

  1. Before the session. Confirm every member knows the schedule and their ring assignment.
  2. Mode B, Day 4. Confirm everyone has pushed, and report anyone who has not the same day — not on Day 5, when it is too late to reassign a partner.
  3. At the session. Confirm attendance and report absences immediately.
  4. After. Review the ten evaluation forms for evidence quality, return those that fall short, and submit the one-page team summary.

Reviewing a form

You check one thing: whether each rating cites specific, checkable evidence — not whether you agree with it. A harsh rating with evidence is forwarded. A generous rating without evidence is returned.

“He understood the problem well and his response was good.”
Return. Nothing here can be checked by anyone who was not present.
“His Response identified that the reused buffer would be overwritten once rows arrived out of order, and pointed to the loop where this occurs.”
Forward. Specific, and checkable against the Response itself.

You must not

  • Assign, adjust, negotiate or predict any mark — any assurance you give will not bind the faculty-in-charge.
  • Decide whether a member used AI. Record the specific observation and leave it there.
  • Do any part of a member's work, however far behind they are. Report it instead.
  • Amend a rating. You may return a form; you may not change one.
  • Withhold an unfavourable observation to protect the team.

A short, exact summary naming one real difficulty is worth considerably more than a long one reporting that all was well.

Eight percent of your own mark is the quality of your audit work — the evaluations you write, and your supervision in the exercise you lead.

AnnexSetting up an audited exercise

For the faculty-in-charge. Nothing in Sections I–X changes between activities; only the following are prepared afresh.

  1. Complete the Activity Details panel and post it to Canvas.
  2. Write the Common Modification — generic in wording, answerable only against the student's own design, and altering a constraint. Prepared before release, not on the day; this is what makes results comparable.
  3. Prepare two default Modifications for members left without one.
  4. Rotate the ring by one position from the previous audited exercise.
  5. Reserve the time: Mode A, one continuous session of about two hours; Mode B, fifteen minutes at release and a protected sixty-minute session on Day 5.

Choosing the exercise and mode

A suitable exercise has constraints that can be altered — assumptions about input validity, scale, data shape or arrival order. One with a single prescribed output and nothing to remove yields no usable probe.

Mode A wherever it fits the hour. Observing the implementation is stronger evidence than inferring it from a commit history afterwards. Mode B is the concession for larger problems, not the default — but it allows the deliberation that analysing a substantial problem needs, so the two together give a fuller picture than either alone.

Frequency. Any number of exercises may be audited in a term. Teams stand; only the ring rotates. The dry run is held once, before the first.
